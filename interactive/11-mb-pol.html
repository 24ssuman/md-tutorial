MB-pol: Water from Many-Body Quantum Chemistry

Module 10 ended with a puzzle: fitting a few parameters to bulk water gives a model that is good at what it was fitted to and fragile elsewhere. MB-pol takes the opposite route. It is built from accurate quantum-chemistry calculations on small clusters (monomers, dimers, trimers) and then used, with no further adjustment to liquid water, to predict clusters, the liquid, the vapour-liquid critical point and ice.

Prerequisites Modules 3, 9 and 10 What you do Decompose cluster energies into 2-, 3- and 4-body terms; compare classical and quantum-nuclei results Expected time to finish About 55 minutes

What you will learn

  • How many parameters it has, what it is fitted to, and (importantly) what it is not fitted to.
  • What it reproduces from the dimer to the liquid, why classical nuclei are not enough, and what it cannot do.

Lesson 1The Many-Body Expansion

Every force field you have used so far is a sum over pairs (plus bonded terms). A pair potential assumes that the energy of three molecules is the sum of the three pair energies. For water that is not true: the electric field of molecule A polarises molecule B, which changes the field that molecule C feels. The right way to ask how far this goes is the many-body expansion (MBE). For $N$ molecules,

$$ E_N = \sum_i E_{1}(i) + \sum_{iwhere the terms are defined recursively by subtracting everything of lower order:

$$ \Delta E_2(i,j) = E(i,j) - E(i) - E(j), \qquad \Delta E_3(i,j,k) = E(i,j,k) - \Delta E_2(i,j) - \Delta E_2(j,k) - \Delta E_2(i,k) - E(i) - E(j) - E(k). $$

Here $E(i,j,k)$ is the energy of those three molecules alone, computed with a quantum-chemistry method; $\Delta E_3$ is the part of it that cannot be explained by pairs. The expansion is exact if carried to order $N$, and its value is that, for water, it converges quickly: in hydrogen-bonded water the two-body term provides roughly 70–75 % of the binding energy, the three-body term roughly 20–25 %, and everything beyond that a few per cent at most, mostly long-range induction [8]. The three-body term is not a small correction: in the water hexamer, for instance, a pairwise model cannot get the energy ordering of the compact three-dimensional cage and prism isomers against the flat ring, whereas including three-body interactions can [9].

This suggests a strategy. Compute the one-, two- and three-body terms accurately with a quantum-chemistry method, fit each to a function that can be evaluated cheaply, treat the (small) four-body and higher terms through a physical model of the long-range induction that causes most of them, and add them up. That is what MB-pol does.

In the lab above, switching the polarisability off leaves the Lennard-Jones and permanent-charge interactions. Why are the three-body and higher terms then exactly zero?

  1. Because the program only computes pairs when polarisability is off.
  2. Because a sum of pair interactions is, by definition, pairwise additive: the energy of any group of molecules is the sum of its pair energies, so $\Delta E_{n\ge3} = 0$.
  3. Because three-body interactions only exist in clusters with fewer than five molecules.
The inclusion–exclusion procedure computes the full energy of every subset. If the energy of a set is just the sum over its pairs, subtracting all the pair terms leaves nothing. Permanent charges and Lennard-Jones are pair interactions. Induction is not: the dipole induced on molecule $i$ depends on the induced dipoles of all the others, so it couples every molecule to every other and generates three-body and higher terms.

Lesson 2The Anatomy of MB-pol

MB-pol (“many-body polarisable”) is a potential energy function for water built in exactly this way [2, 3, 4, 6]. It is full-dimensional (the molecules are flexible) and combines machine-learned short-range terms with physically motivated long-range ones:

What is a permutationally invariant polynomial?

A potential energy surface must not change if you swap two identical atoms, for example the two hydrogens of one molecule or the two molecules of a dimer. Writing a flexible function of the interatomic distances that respects this symmetry exactly is the job of permutationally invariant polynomials [17]. Take the distances between all pairs of atoms, transform each into a bounded variable $x_{ab} = \exp(-k_{ab}\, r_{ab})$, and form all monomials in the $x_{ab}$ up to a given degree, symmetrised by summing over all permutations of identical atoms. The potential is a linear combination of these symmetrised monomials, $V_\text{poly} = \sum_l c_l\, m_l(\mathbf x)$. The coefficients $c_l$ are linear parameters, so given the training data they are found by (regularised) linear least squares; the ranges $k_{ab}$ are nonlinear parameters found by a simplex search [2].

The long-range pieces

  • Permanent electrostatics. Point charges on the two hydrogens and a massless M site (as in TIP4P, Module 10), but with charges that change with the geometry of the molecule, taken from an accurate dipole-moment surface [2].
  • Dispersion. A $C_6/r^6$ interaction between all atom pairs, damped at short range by a Tang–Toennies function [18]. The three $C_6$ coefficients (HH, OH, OO) are fitted to long-range dimer energies and constrained so that they sum to the correct total dispersion of the molecule pair [2].
  • Induction. Each atom carries an isotropic polarisability; the induced dipoles interact with the permanent charges and with each other through a damped dipole–dipole tensor (the Thole damping [16]), and the whole set is solved self-consistently at every step. This is the same idea as the toy model in the lab above and it is what makes the potential many-body.

Two cut-offs keep the cost down: the short-range two-body PIP is switched off smoothly between 5.5 and 7.5 Å (oxygen–oxygen distance), and the three-body PIP at 4.5 Å [2, 3].

So roughly 2300 linear coefficients and a few dozen nonlinear parameters, all fitted to gas-phase quantum-chemistry calculations on two and three molecules. MB-pol was not fitted to any experimental property of liquid water: every liquid, ice and interface result below is a prediction [6]. On the same set of 12,347 coupled-cluster trimers, the three-body error of MB-pol is 0.028 kcal/mol per trimer, against 0.104 for the fitted surface WHBB, 0.158 for HBB2-pol and 0.313 for the polarisable iAMOEBA-rev5 model [3].

💡 Deep dive: how the potential was later refined

The 2023 update, MB-pol(2023), keeps the same physical core and offers higher-degree two- and three-body polynomials (up to 3828 and 2207 coefficients), a trained explicit four-body term (266 coefficients), and larger training sets (71,892 dimers, 45,332 trimers, 3,692 tetramers) [11]. A recent variant replaces the polynomials by permutationally invariant Fourier series for better robustness at short range [12]. The software used to fit such potentials from new quantum-chemistry data (MB-Fit) and to evaluate them in molecular dynamics (MBX) are described in [14] and [13].

Lesson 3What MB-pol Reproduces: From the Dimer to the Liquid

The evidence is staged by system size. Each stage tests the potential on something it was not fitted to.

  • The dimer. Twelve-dimensional quantum bound-state calculations of the vibration–rotation–tunnelling spectrum of (H₂O)₂ and (D₂O)₂ agree with experimental tunnelling splittings to within 0.02–0.5 cm⁻¹ [2].
  • The second virial coefficient (the first correction to the ideal gas, set by pair interactions) agrees with experiment from 200 to 600 K, without any adjustment [2]. The third virial coefficient, which probes three-body interactions, was used to validate the trimer term [3].
  • Clusters. MB-pol reproduces the energy ordering of the tested small-cluster isomers (up to the hexamer) within chemical accuracy, with one exception, a pentamer isomer; many-body energy contributions and vibrational frequencies agree closely with coupled-cluster references [3, 5].
  • The liquid. See the lab below and the table.
  • Ice. With classical nuclei the melting temperature of ice Ih is 263.5 K against 273 K in experiment; lattice energies and densities of several ice phases are well reproduced, and the authors attribute the remaining deviations to the omitted nuclear quantum effects [5]. Getting the ordering of the lattice energies of the ice polymorphs right needs every term of the many-body expansion to be accurate, at short and long range [11].
  • Vapour–liquid equilibrium. Direct-coexistence simulations give a critical temperature of 639 ± 14 K and density of 0.34 ± 0.03 g/cm³ for the flexible potential, against the experimental 647.1 K and 0.322 g/cm³ [10]. For comparison, the five rigid fixed-charge models in the Vega–Abascal review (Module 10) span 521–640 K, with SPC/E and TIP4P/2005 also close to experiment [Module 10, ref. 23].
  • Spectra. Infrared and Raman spectra of liquid water from path-integral (centroid) dynamics with MB-pol agree with experiment in line positions and shapes; the remaining error in the O–H stretch band shape is attributed to quantum dynamical effects [11].

Look at the pattern. The static properties (density, heat of vaporisation) are good either way: the classical density is 0.7 % high and the heat of vaporisation 4 % high, and the quantum values straddle the experiment. The dynamics is a different story: with classical nuclei the molecules diffuse and rotate twice as slowly as in reality, and with quantum nuclei they agree with experiment. Quantum delocalisation of the hydrogens weakens the effective hydrogen bonds, and that is invisible to a classical simulation however accurate the potential. This is the strongest argument for treating the nuclei quantum mechanically when the goal is quantitative agreement, and it is why MB-pol is routinely used with PIMD.

Exercise 1 — What does MB-pol buy over a fitted model?

Compare the lab above with Module 10’s “models versus experiment” lab. (a) For the three properties that appear in both, which fitted rigid model comes closest to experiment, and how does MB-pol (PIMD) compare? (b) MB-pol has about 2300 fitted coefficients, TIP4P/2005 has about half a dozen parameters. Why is the larger model less likely to be an artefact of fitting?

One possible answer

(a) For the density TIP4P/2005 (0.993) and SPC/E (0.994) are within 0.5 % of experiment and MB-pol is within 0.4 %; for the heat of vaporisation the raw SPC/E and TIP4P/2005 values are 12–14 % too high (before the polarisation correction) while MB-pol with PIMD is within 4 %; for diffusion SPC/E (2.54) and TIP4P/2005 (2.06) are within about 10 % and MB-pol with PIMD (2.2) within about 5 %. The point is not that MB-pol wins every row, it is that it matches comparably well without having been told any of these numbers. (b) A model is an artefact of fitting when it reproduces its targets by compensating errors. MB-pol’s coefficients are constrained by tens of thousands of quantum-chemistry energies of small clusters, whose physics (exchange repulsion, induction, dispersion) is the same physics that governs the liquid, so it transfers: the liquid properties are predictions, not fit results. TIP4P/2005’s few parameters were adjusted to the bulk properties themselves.

Lesson 4Limits and Practical Use

  • Cost. MB-pol is more expensive than a rigid force field because of the polynomial evaluations and the self-consistent induction, and a path-integral simulation multiplies the cost by the number of beads [6]. It is nevertheless far cheaper than ab initio MD with coupled-cluster accuracy (which is out of reach).
  • It is not reactive. Water molecules keep their identity: MB-pol cannot dissociate into H₃O⁺ and OH⁻, so processes such as proton transfer need a reactive model or ab initio MD [6].
  • Charge transfer is represented only implicitly, through the short-range polynomial fits [6].
  • Systematic improvability. This is the design goal: if a property is wrong, one can identify which term is responsible (two-body? three-body? induction?) and improve it with more data, as in MB-pol(2023) with its four-body term [11].

Running it

MB-pol is distributed as part of MBX, a C++ library that evaluates the many-body energies and forces and interfaces with molecular dynamics engines including i-PI (for path-integral simulations), LAMMPS, OpenMM and AMBER [13]. New many-body potentials of this kind are fitted from quantum-chemistry data with MB-Fit [14]. The workflow, in outline: (1) prepare a water box; (2) point your MD engine (or i-PI, for quantum nuclei) at MBX; (3) run NVT or NPT dynamics exactly as in Modules 9 and 12, with a time step small enough for a flexible model (a fraction of a femtosecond). Installation and the interface to each engine are described in the MBX paper and its documentation, which we do not reproduce here.

End of Module 11. MB-pol showed a different philosophy of force-field building:

  • Expand the energy in the number of interacting molecules and compute each order accurately from quantum chemistry.
  • Fit cheap functions to the one-, two- and three-body terms, and treat the rest through physically motivated induction.
  • Test the result, with no further adjustment, on the dimer, clusters, the liquid, ice and the critical point.
  • Combine it with path-integral nuclei to get the dynamics right.

📝 Key Takeaways

  • The many-body expansion splits the energy into one-, two-, three-… body terms; for water the two-body term gives about 70–75 % of the binding energy and the three-body term about 20–25 %, so pair potentials are not enough.
  • Permanent charges and Lennard-Jones are pairwise additive; induction (polarisation) is what generates the many-body terms.
  • MB-pol combines a monomer surface, short-range two- and three-body permutationally invariant polynomials fitted to CCSD(T) data on dimers and trimers, and long-range electrostatics, dispersion and Thole-damped induction.
  • It is fitted to gas-phase clusters only, never to liquid-water experiments, so liquid, ice and critical-point results are predictions.
  • With classical nuclei the dynamics are too slow by a factor of two; with path-integral nuclei the same potential matches the experimental diffusion and rotation.
