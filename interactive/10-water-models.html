The Water-Model Zoo

SPC/E is one of dozens of water models. Some have four or five interaction sites, some are flexible, some polarise, some treat the nuclei quantum mechanically, and some are not force fields at all. This module is a guided tour: what each family adds, where its parameters come from, and how well it reproduces experiment, with the original paper for every model.

Prerequisites Modules 3 and 9 What you do Compare model geometries and charges, and check models against experiment Expected time to finish About 50 minutes

What you will learn

  • The main families of water models and what physics each one adds.
  • How three-, four- and five-site rigid models place their charges, and why.
  • What flexible, polarisable and “quantum” water models mean.
  • How the parameters of a model are obtained (in outline, without refitting anything).
  • How rigid non-polarisable models compare with experiment, and why no single model wins.

Lesson 1Why So Many Models? A Short History

Water is a small molecule but a hard liquid to model. It forms a hydrogen-bond network that is strong, directional and cooperative; it has a density maximum at 4 °C, an unusually large dielectric constant (78), and a heat capacity, compressibility and viscosity that all behave anomalously on cooling. A model that gets all of this right for the right reasons is still an open problem, and the history of water models is a history of adding ingredients one by one.

  • 1933: Bernal and Fowler propose a rigid molecule with point charges and use it to explain the structure of water and of ionic solutions [1].
  • 1971: Rahman and Stillinger run the first molecular dynamics simulation of liquid water [2]. This is the same Aneesur Rahman whose 1964 simulation of argon you recreated in Module 8: seven years after the argon liquid, he and Frank Stillinger turned the method on water. Three years later came ST2 [3], a rigid five-site model with a tetrahedral arrangement of charges.
  • 1981–1983: the simple point-charge models that became the workhorses of biomolecular simulation: SPC [4], and TIP3P and TIP4P [6]. They are cheap because they are rigid and non-polarisable, and they were fitted to a handful of liquid properties.
  • 1987–2005: refinements within the same philosophy: SPC/E [5], TIP5P [7], TIP4P/Ew [8], TIP4P/2005 and TIP4P/Ice [9, 10]. The aim moves from “a liquid that looks like water” to “the right density anomaly” and “the right phase diagram”.
  • 1994 onwards: models that polarise, so that each molecule responds to the electric field of its neighbours [16–19].
  • 2006 onwards: flexible models for spectroscopy [13, 14, 15], models for nuclear quantum effects [14], and, in the last decade, models fitted directly to quantum-chemistry data, from many-body potentials such as MB-pol (Module 11) to machine-learned potentials [21].

Lesson 2Rigid Non-Polarisable Models: 3, 4 and 5 Sites

These models keep the molecule rigid, give it a fixed charge distribution and place a single Lennard-Jones centre on oxygen (Module 9). They differ in where the charges sit.

3 sites

SPC, SPC/E, TIP3P, OPC3

Charges on O and the two H. The negative charge sits on the oxygen nucleus. Cheapest. SPC [4], SPC/E [5], TIP3P [6], OPC3 [12].

4 sites

TIP4P family, OPC

The negative charge moves to a massless site M on the bisector, 0.10–0.16 Å from oxygen towards the hydrogens. TIP4P [6], TIP4P/Ew [8], TIP4P/2005 [9], TIP4P/Ice [10], OPC [11].

5 sites

TIP5P (and ST2)

Two positive charges on H and two negative charges on “lone-pair” sites L 0.70 Å from O, in the plane perpendicular to the molecule, giving a tetrahedral charge arrangement. TIP5P [7], ST2 [3].

Why move the charge off the oxygen? The same total charge at a different position changes the molecule’s quadrupole moment (the next term after the dipole in the multipole expansion), and it is the dipole and quadrupole together that determine how the molecules orient in the field of their neighbours, i.e. the hydrogen-bond geometry. Moving the negative charge from O to M changes how the electrostatic potential falls off around the molecule at the same total charge and the same geometry, and it is this that the four-site models exploit to improve the hydrogen-bond structure; the lone-pair sites of TIP5P give the most tetrahedral arrangement. The price is a more complicated force calculation: the massless M or L sites have no mass of their own, so the force on them is redistributed onto the real atoms at every step (ASE’s TIP4P calculator does exactly this via its add_virtual_sites and redistribute_forces methods).

TIP4P moves the negative charge from the oxygen to a massless site M on the bisector. Which statement is correct?

  1. M carries mass, so the model needs a smaller time step.
  2. M carries no mass; its position is computed from the three real atoms at every step and the force on it is passed back to them.
  3. M is a Lennard-Jones centre that replaces the oxygen.
M is a “virtual site”: a charge at a position that is a fixed linear combination of the O and H positions. Because it has no mass it adds no degrees of freedom; the force on it is split among the three atoms by the same combination (the chain rule). In TIP4P the Lennard-Jones centre stays on the oxygen.

Lesson 3Beyond Rigid: Flexible, Polarisable and “Quantum” Models

Flexible models

Rigid models cannot describe the infrared spectrum, the response of the bonds to the neighbours, or, together with the quantum treatment of the nuclei (below), the effect of hydrogen bonding on the O–H stretch. Flexible models add bond and angle terms (Module 9). SPC/Fw [13] is a flexible SPC-like model whose parameters were adjusted to reproduce bulk diffusion and the dielectric constant. q-TIP4P/F [14] is a flexible TIP4P-like model designed for path-integral simulations, with an anharmonic O–H stretch; TIP4P/2005f [15] is a flexible version of TIP4P/2005 with a Morse bond.

Polarisable models

A real molecule’s charge distribution responds to the electric field of its surroundings: the dipole is 1.85 D in the gas phase and about 2.5–3 D in the liquid. A fixed-charge model can only represent the average (SPC/E’s trick). Polarisable models let the charge distribution respond. There are three main ways:

  • Induced point dipoles (Thole-type): each atom carries a polarisability and acquires an induced dipole $\boldsymbol\mu_i = \alpha_i \mathbf E_i$ that is solved self-consistently. The TTM3-F model [19] is of this type; AMOEBA water [18] combines induced dipoles with permanent multipoles up to the quadrupole.
  • Drude oscillators: a light, charged “Drude particle” is attached to each heavy atom by a spring; its displacement is the induced dipole. SWM4-NDP [17] is a Drude model.
  • Fluctuating charges: the partial charges themselves change to equalise the electronegativity, as in TIP4P-FQ [16].

Polarisable models typically cost several times more than rigid ones because the induced dipoles must be solved at every step, but they transfer better between environments (bulk, interface, ion solutions).

“Quantum” water: two different meanings

Two kinds of “quantum”

(i) Quantum nuclei. Hydrogen is light: its zero-point energy, tunnelling and delocalised wave function are not negligible at 300 K. Path-integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) represents each quantum nucleus as a ring polymer of classical beads (the idea is the subject of Tuckerman’s textbook). It is exact for static properties in the limit of many beads and costs the number of beads times a classical run. Models such as q-TIP4P/F [14] are fitted for this setting, and some classical models are tuned to mimic nuclear quantum effects implicitly. The effect is large: for the MB-pol model (Module 11), classical dynamics gives a diffusion constant of 0.12 Å²/ps and a rotational relaxation time of 5.3 ps, while PIMD with the same potential gives 0.22 Å²/ps and 2.3 ps, against experiment 0.23 Å²/ps and 2.5 ps [25].

(ii) Quantum electrons. Instead of a force field, compute the forces from the electronic structure at every step: ab initio MD, pioneered by Car and Parrinello [20], usually with density functional theory. It has no fitted water parameters but costs thousands of times more than a force field and inherits the errors of the chosen functional. Machine-learned potentials (for example Deep Potential [21]) train a neural network on electronic-structure energies and forces and then run at close to force-field cost. MB-pol (Module 11) sits in between: a physically structured potential fitted to coupled-cluster data.

Coarse-grained water

At the other extreme, the mW model [22] represents a water molecule as a single particle interacting through a Stillinger–Weber-type potential that favours tetrahedral angles. It has no charges and no hydrogens, is two orders of magnitude cheaper than all-atom water, and reproduces the structure, density anomaly and melting of water surprisingly well, but it cannot represent dipoles, the dielectric constant or the spectrum.

Lesson 4Where Do the Parameters Come From?

A water model has few parameters: a geometry ($r_\mathrm{OH}$, $\theta$), a charge $q_\mathrm H$ (the oxygen charge follows from neutrality), the Lennard-Jones $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$, perhaps an M-site distance. Four ways of choosing them cover almost everything in the table above.

  1. Fit to experimental bulk properties (the classical approach). Choose the targets (density, heat of vaporisation, diffusion, dielectric constant, the temperature of maximum density, the melting point of ice) and adjust the few parameters until a simulation reproduces them. Every model in Lesson 2 was made this way; they differ in which properties they target. SPC and TIP3P/TIP4P target the density and the heat of vaporisation at room temperature [4, 6]; TIP5P targets the density anomaly [7]; TIP4P/2005 targets the temperature of maximum density and the ice phases [9]; TIP4P/Ice targets the melting temperature [10]. A model fitted for one property tends to do worse on the others, which is why different models are recommended for different uses.
  2. Fit to molecular properties first (OPC). Rather than fitting bulk properties directly, optimise the model’s multipole moments and then the remaining parameters [11, 12].
  3. Fit to quantum-chemistry data on small clusters (TTM, AMOEBA, MB-pol). Compute the interaction energies of water monomers, dimers, trimers and larger clusters with high-level methods (MP2, CCSD(T)) and adjust a physically motivated functional form to reproduce them, sometimes with a final adjustment against liquid properties [18, 19]. MB-pol (Module 11) does not use any liquid-phase experimental data at all.
  4. Learn a potential from electronic-structure data (machine-learned potentials). A neural network or other flexible function is trained on thousands of energies and forces from density functional theory or coupled cluster [21]. There are no physical parameters, but there are thousands of fitted weights.

Two further points. First, fixed-charge models fitted to liquid properties are effective potentials: because they are fitted to the liquid, their charges include an average polarisation, so they overbind in the gas phase (SPC/E’s energy correction, Module 12). Second, an experimentally fitted model is only as good as its targets are comprehensive: the next lesson shows what happens outside the fitted range.

Lesson 5How Good Are They? Models Against Experiment

Vega and Abascal compared five widely used rigid non-polarisable models with experiment on 17 groups of properties spanning vapour, liquid and ice, and published the simulation results in a table [23]. Several lessons come out of that comparison [23]:

  • No rigid non-polarisable model wins everywhere. The review reports that improving the match with one set of properties tends to cost accuracy on another and that none of the tested models reproduces all of the gas, liquid, solid and high-pressure properties at once.
  • Phase behaviour is hard. For most rigid non-polarisable models the melting temperature of ice Ih lies about 25 K below the temperature of maximum density, against 4 K in experiment, with TIP5P as the exception; compare the Tm and TMD plots above.
  • Missing physics. The authors point to polarisation, many-body interactions and nuclear quantum effects as the physics that fixed-charge classical models leave out [23].
  • Different models for different jobs. TIP3P (modified) is the water of the CHARMM and AMBER biomolecular force fields because the proteins were parameterised with it; TIP4P/2005 is a good general-purpose rigid model for the condensed phases; TIP5P and TIP4P/Ew are tuned for the density anomaly and for PME respectively.
Read the table with care

The heat of vaporisation for the SPC/E model in the plot (11.79 kcal/mol) is the raw value from the simulation. SPC/E’s charges already include an average polarisation, whose energy cost must be subtracted (Module 12); with that correction SPC/E agrees with experiment (10.52 kcal/mol) almost exactly, as designed. The review’s values also come from simulations of different sizes and lengths; the diffusion constants in particular depend on finite-size corrections. The differences between models on the dielectric constant (50 to 94, against 78.5) are much larger than any such uncertainty.

Exercise 3 — Pick a model for the job

Using the lab above and the table in Lesson 2, which model would you choose for (a) a protein in water with the AMBER force field, (b) a study of the freezing of ice, (c) a study of the density anomaly of liquid water near 4 °C, (d) the rate of hydrogen-bond switching (which depends on the diffusion constant)? Justify each choice with at least one number from the lab.

One possible answer
  • (a) TIP3P (in its CHARMM/AMBER form): the protein parameters were derived together with it, so consistency matters more than the water’s own accuracy, even though it gives a diffusion constant more than twice the experimental one (5.5 vs 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ cm²/s in the lab) and a poor melting point.
  • (b) TIP4P/Ice, which was fitted to the ice melting temperature [10]; among the models in the lab TIP5P (274 K) and TIP4P/2005 (252 K) come closest to the experimental 273.15 K, the others lie 40–130 K too low.
  • (c) TIP4P/2005 (278 K) or TIP5P (277 K): their maximum-density temperatures are within 1 K of the experimental 277 K, whereas TIP3P gives 182 K.
  • (d) SPC/E or TIP4P/2005: their diffusion constants (2.5 and 2.1 × 10⁻⁵ cm²/s) are within about 10 % of the experimental 2.3, while TIP3P (5.5) and TIP4P (3.2) are far too fast.

📝 Key Takeaways

  • The simplest water models differ in the position of their charges: on the atoms (3 sites), on a massless M site (4 sites) or on lone-pair sites (5 sites). A model’s dipole follows directly from its geometry and charges.
  • Most models are effective: a handful of parameters fitted to chosen bulk properties, so they are accurate for the targets and less so elsewhere. Different models target different properties.
  • Flexible, polarisable and quantum-nuclei treatments each add physics that fixed-charge rigid models leave out, at higher cost.
  • “Quantum” can mean quantum nuclei (path integrals) or quantum electrons (ab initio MD, machine-learned potentials trained on quantum data).
  • No rigid non-polarisable model reproduces the properties of water across vapour, liquid and ice, which motivates systematically improvable many-body potentials (Module 11).
