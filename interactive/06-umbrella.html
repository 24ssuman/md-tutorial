# Bias potential and force for umbrella sampling
def potential_bias_us(x, k_us, x0_us):
    """Harmonic umbrella sampling bias potential energy."""
    return 0.5 * k_us * (x - x0_us)**2


def force_bias_us(x, k_us, x0_us):
    """Force from harmonic umbrella sampling bias."""
    return -k_us * (x - x0_us)


def Velocity_verlet_langevin_US(x_curr, v_curr, mass, dt, gamma, target_temp,
                                 force_physical_func, force_bias_func,
                                 physical_args={}, bias_args={}):
    """
    Langevin dynamics (BAOAB) with physical + bias forces for umbrella sampling.
    """
    f_phys_curr = force_physical_func(x_curr, **physical_args)
    f_bias_curr = force_bias_func(x_curr, **bias_args)
    f_total_curr = f_phys_curr + f_bias_curr

    v_half_b = v_curr + (f_total_curr / mass) * (dt / 2.0)
    x_half_a = x_curr + v_half_b * (dt / 2.0)

    c1 = np.exp(-gamma * dt)
    c2 = np.sqrt(k_B * max(0, target_temp) * (1.0 - c1**2) / mass)
    random_kick = np.random.normal(0, 1)
    v_half_o = c1 * v_half_b + c2 * random_kick

    x_new = x_half_a + v_half_o * (dt / 2.0)

    f_phys_new = force_physical_func(x_new, **physical_args)
    f_bias_new = force_bias_func(x_new, **bias_args)
    f_total_new = f_phys_new + f_bias_new

    v_new = v_half_o + (f_total_new / mass) * (dt / 2.0)
    return x_new, v_new

Lesson 1The Idea Behind Biasing

The core idea behind many enhanced sampling methods is to modify the potential energy landscape the system experiences during the simulation. We add an artificial bias potential, $V_{bias}(q)$, which depends on our chosen collective variable $q$ (in our case, $q=x$).

The total potential energy used to calculate forces in the simulation becomes:

$$ V_{total}(x) = V_{physical}(x) + V_{bias}(x) $$

where $V_{physical}(x)$ is the original double-well potential.

The corresponding total force is:

$$ F_{total}(x) = F_{physical}(x) + F_{bias}(x) $$

where $F_{bias}(x) = -\frac{dV_{bias}}{dx}$.

By carefully choosing $V_{bias}(x)$, we can make high-energy regions (like the barrier top) more favorable to visit, effectively “flattening” the energy landscape locally and encouraging transitions.

The catch: biased data

Of course, this simulation runs on a modified potential. The data we collect (histograms, averages) are biased. The crucial second step is to mathematically remove the effect of the bias potential afterwards to recover the properties of the original, unbiased system, such as the true PMF. Umbrella Sampling uses a specific form of bias and a method called WHAM to achieve this.

Play with a bias

The lab below shows the exact (Boltzmann) consequences of adding a bias. Pick a barrier-flattening bias to see the “flattening” idea, or a harmonic umbrella (the bias used in the rest of this module). The dotted curve multiplies the biased distribution by $e^{+V_{bias}(x)/k_BT}$ and renormalizes — that is the “mathematically remove the bias” step, and it lands exactly on the unbiased distribution.

A biased simulation samples $V_{physical} + V_{bias}$. Why can't we read the PMF straight off its histogram?

  1. Because Langevin dynamics never reaches equilibrium
  2. Because the histogram has too few bins
  3. Because the histogram reflects the modified landscape; the bias must be removed afterwards (reweighting / WHAM)
The biased histogram follows $e^{-[V_{physical}+V_{bias}]/k_BT}$. Removing the known bias factor $e^{-V_{bias}/k_BT}$ — exactly what the dotted curve in the lab does, and what WHAM does across many windows — recovers the unbiased distribution.

Lesson 2Harmonic Umbrella Potentials

Umbrella Sampling uses a series of simulations, often called windows. In each window $i$, a harmonic bias potential is applied and centered at a target value $x_{0,i}$ along the collective variable $x$.

The bias potential in window $i$ is:

$$ V_{\mathrm{bias},i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{us}}\,(x-x_{0,i})^2 $$

This potential is a harmonic (parabolic) restraint (an “umbrella”) centered at $x_{0,i}$.

$k_{\mathrm{us}}$

The spring constant of the umbrella potential. Larger $k_{\mathrm{us}}$ means tighter sampling around the center.

$x_{0,i}$

The center of the harmonic restraint for window $i$.

The corresponding biasing force is:

$$ F_{\mathrm{bias},i}(x)=-\frac{dV_{\mathrm{bias},i}}{dx}=-k_{\mathrm{us}}(x-x_{0,i}) $$

By running multiple windows with centers spanning the full region of interest (from one well, across the barrier, to the other well), we can sample high-energy regions effectively. A key requirement is good overlap between neighboring window histograms.

Switch the lab above to Harmonic umbrella and drag $x_0$ from $-2$ to $+2$: each position is one window. With $k_{\mathrm{us}} = 60$ the biased landscape has a single minimum near $x_0$ even at the barrier top, so the window samples a narrow, roughly Gaussian slice of $x$.

What you should see

(1) Four windows are about 1.33 apart, but each histogram is only a few tenths wide: gaps open between neighbours, the nearest-neighbour overlap drops to essentially zero and the reconstructed PMF is broken or wildly offset. (2) A stiff spring narrows every window (width $\sigma \approx \sqrt{k_BT/k_{\rm eff}}$), so even 10 windows leave gaps. (3) A soft spring gives broad, strongly overlapping windows, and WHAM converges in a few hundred iterations instead of a few thousand. The price is weak restraint: near the barrier top the double-well curvature ($V''(0) = -2b = -8$) almost cancels $k_{\rm us} = 10$, so the central windows wander over a wide range instead of focusing on their own slice, and for $k_{\rm us} < 8$ the window centred on the barrier would itself become a double well. Overlap is what WHAM needs; tune $N$ and $k_{\rm us}$ together.

Your histogram overlay shows a clear gap between windows 5 and 6. Which change is the right fix?

  1. Increase $k_{\rm us}$ so each window is better defined
  2. Add windows in the gap, or decrease $k_{\rm us}$ so the windows broaden
  3. Run WHAM for more iterations
A gap means no window samples that region, so WHAM has nothing to connect the two sides with. Only more (or broader) sampling there fixes it; extra WHAM iterations cannot create data. A stiffer spring makes the gap worse.

Lesson 4PMF Reconstruction with WHAM

We now have the biased position data from each window ($i=1...N_{win}$). We need to combine this data to get the unbiased PMF using the Weighted Histogram Analysis Method (WHAM).

WHAM equations. The core idea is to find the unbiased probability distribution $P(x)$ and the free energy offsets $f_i$ for each window that are self-consistent with the observed data. The iterative equations are:

1. Update the unbiased probability $P(x_j)$ (for each bin $j$):

$$P(x_j) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{win}} N_{i,j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{win}} n_i \exp\left(\frac{f_i - V_{bias,i}(x_j)}{k_B T}\right)}$$

2. Update the free energy offsets $f_i$ (for each window $i$):

$$ \exp\left(-\frac{f_i}{k_B T}\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{N_{bins}} P(x_j) \exp\left(-\frac{V_{bias,i}(x_j)}{k_B T}\right) $$

Where:

  • $N_{win}$ is the number of windows.
  • $N_{bins}$ is the number of bins in our histogram.
  • $N_{i,j}$ is the number of counts (samples) from window $i$ that fall into global bin $j$.
  • $n_i$ is the total number of production samples in window $i$.
  • $V_{bias,i}(x_j)$ is the value of the bias potential for window $i$ evaluated at the center $x_j$ of bin $j$.
  • $f_i$ is the free energy offset for window $i$ (relative to some reference, often $f_0=0$).
  • $P(x_j)$ is the estimate of the unbiased probability in bin $j$.

We iterate these two equations until the values of $f_i$ (or $P(x_j)$) converge.

Implementing WHAM

Here's a function to perform the iterative WHAM calculation.

⚠️ Crucial step: evaluating window overlap

For WHAM to successfully reconstruct a smooth PMF, adjacent umbrella sampling windows must overlap significantly.

  • Good overlap: The histograms of adjacent windows overlap, meaning a particle at the edge of one window occasionally experiences the bias of the neighboring window. This allows WHAM to align their free energies.
  • Bad overlap (too few windows or too stiff spring): If histograms do not overlap (leaving gaps between them), WHAM has no data to connect the windows, resulting in severe errors or numerical failure.

If you see a gap in your histogram plot, you must either add more windows at the gap or reduce the spring constant ($k$) to allow broader sampling!

def run_wham(N_ij, n_i, reduced_bias_energies_ij, max_iter=10000, tolerance=1e-7, verbose=True):

    Wrapper around the package WHAM solver to match the API used in this module.
    """
    P_j, f_i, _ = _run_wham(N_ij, n_i, reduced_bias_energies_ij, max_iter=max_iter, tol=tolerance, verbose=verbose)

# --- Calculate PMF from WHAM Probability ---
pmf_wham = None
if P_j_wham is not None:
    # Calculate PMF = -kT * ln(P_j)
    # Avoid log(0) for bins with zero probability
    pmf_wham = np.full_like(P_j_wham, np.inf) # Initialize with infinity
    valid_bins_pmf = P_j_wham > 1e-12
    with warnings.catch_warnings():
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", category=RuntimeWarning)
        pmf_wham[valid_bins_pmf] = -k_B * temperature * np.log(P_j_wham[valid_bins_pmf])

    # Shift PMF so its minimum is zero
    min_pmf_val = np.min(pmf_wham[np.isfinite(pmf_wham)])
    if np.isfinite(min_pmf_val):
        pmf_wham -= min_pmf_val
        print(f"\nPMF calculated. Min value shifted to 0.")
    else:
        print("\nWarning: Could not find finite minimum PMF value. PMF might be invalid.")

         print(f"PMF Barrier Height (Custom WHAM, approx): {pmf_barrier_max:.2f}")
    else:
         print("Could not estimate PMF barrier height (no finite points in barrier region).")

    plt.text(0, barrier_height / 2, "PMF Calculation Failed or Skipped.", ha='center', color='red', fontsize=12)

Convergence with simulation length

After reconstructing the PMF with WHAM on the full dataset, verify convergence by running WHAM on increasing subsets of the umbrella trajectories. We vary the number of frames per window ($M$ = 100, 200, … up to the full length) and track:

  • The free-energy difference between the two minima (ΔG_minima)
  • The free-energy barrier height (ΔG_barrier)
Important points to note
  • Physically relevant points: In a real-world scenario, you would carefully choose the points for your convergence analysis based on the specific system you are studying and the quantities you want to compute. It could be the free energy difference between two states, the barrier height between them, or other properties of interest.
  • Assumptions: In this example, we've assumed knowledge of the minima positions and barrier position for the double-well potential. In practice, you might need to use other techniques to find these points beforehand.
  • Convergence indicators: We're still tracking the deltaG_minima and deltaG_barrier_min1 values to assess convergence. You might need to monitor other quantities depending on your system and requirements.

Plotting these as a function of $M$ shows whether the PMF has stabilized.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

max_frames = min(len(p) for p in all_positions_production)
nsteps = 200
step_size  = max(1, max_frames // nsteps)
steps      = list(range(step_size, max_frames+1, step_size))
if steps[-1] != max_frames:
    steps.append(max_frames)

# Precompute target indices
min1_pos    = -np.sqrt(b_dw / (2 * a_dw))
min2_pos    =  np.sqrt(b_dw / (2 * a_dw))
barrier_pos =  0.0

idx_min1    = np.argmin(np.abs(bin_centers_global - min1_pos))
idx_min2    = np.argmin(np.abs(bin_centers_global - min2_pos))
idx_barrier = np.argmin(np.abs(bin_centers_global - barrier_pos))

# Containers
deltaG_minima       = []
deltaG_barrier_min1 = []
deltaG_barrier_min2 = []

# --- Convergence loop ---
for M in steps:
    # build counts up to frame M for each window
    N_ij_M = np.array([
        np.histogram(pos[:M], bins=bin_edges_global)[0]
        for pos in all_positions_production
    ])
    n_i_M = N_ij_M.sum(axis=1)

    # run WHAM
    P_j_M, _ = run_wham(N_ij_M, n_i_M, reduced_bias_energies_ij,
                        max_iter=10000, tolerance=1e-4,verbose=False)

    # compute PMF
    pmf_M = -k_B * temperature * np.log(P_j_M + 1e-18)

    # record free‐energy differences
    deltaG_minima.append       (pmf_M[idx_min2]    - pmf_M[idx_min1])

With the default windows, read off from the convergence plot roughly how many frames per window you need before the barrier estimate stays within about 0.1 of the true value 4.00. Hint

The curves depend on the number of (correlated) frames per window that went into the histograms. Early on the estimates jump around by several tenths because the window histograms are still noisy, especially in the tails where neighbouring windows overlap. Once those overlap regions are well populated the curves flatten; with fewer steps per window the same plateau is reached only at the right-hand end of a shorter axis — or not at all.

In WHAM, why do we have to iterate instead of solving for $P(x_j)$ in one shot?

  1. The $P(x_j)$ equation needs the offsets $f_i$, and the $f_i$ equation needs $P(x_j)$ — they must be made self-consistent
  2. Because the histograms are recomputed from new simulations every iteration
  3. Because each iteration adds one more umbrella window
The two WHAM equations are coupled. Starting from a guess (all $f_i = 0$), we alternate between them until the $f_i$ stop changing — the max $|\Delta f_i|$ trace in the WHAM lab shows exactly this.

📝 Key Takeaways

  • Umbrella Sampling adds harmonic bias potentials $w_i(x) = \frac{1}{2} k_c (x - x_{c,i})^2$ to restrain the system in high-energy regions.
  • We run multiple independent windows across the reaction coordinate to ensure uniform sampling.
  • The Weighted Histogram Analysis Method (WHAM) mathematically combines the biased histograms, self-consistently subtracting the bias potentials to reconstruct the global unbiased PMF.
  • Always verify histogram overlap before running WHAM to avoid gaps and disconnected profiles.
