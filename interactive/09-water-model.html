Building a Water Model: Bonded Terms and SPC

So far every particle was a single atom. Water is a molecule: two O–H bonds and an H–O–H angle, plus charges that make it stick to its neighbours. This module adds the bonded terms of a force field, builds the classic SPC family of water models, and runs them with ASE.

Prerequisites Modules 2, 3 and 8 What you run A bond, a flexible H₂O molecule, and a box of liquid water (SPC/E and SPC/Fw) Expected time to finish About 60 minutes

What you will learn

  • What bonded terms are (bond stretching and angle bending) and how they set the time step.
  • How a simple water model combines bonded terms, Lennard-Jones on oxygen and partial charges.
  • The difference between rigid (SPC, SPC/E) and flexible (SPC/Fw) models, and how constraints work.
  • How to run water in ASE: vibrational modes of one molecule, then a box of liquid water.

Lesson 1From Atoms to Molecules

In Module 8 the whole force field was one pair potential, Lennard-Jones. A molecule needs more. The classic force-field energy splits into bonded terms, which hold a molecule together, and non-bonded terms, which act between molecules (and between distant parts of one large molecule):

Exclusions: no double counting

Atoms joined by one or two bonds (1–2 and 1–3 pairs) are already described by the bond and angle terms. Their non-bonded interaction is therefore excluded. In water the hydrogens are 1–2 partners of the oxygen and 1–3 partners of each other, so all intramolecular non-bonded interactions are excluded and molecules interact only through their atoms on different molecules. With an Ewald sum (Module 3) the exclusion must also remove the intramolecular part of the reciprocal-space sum: subtract $q_i q_j\,\mathrm{erf}(\alpha r_{ij})/r_{ij}$ for every excluded pair. The code in this module does exactly that.

Lesson 2The Bond Term and the Time Step

Near its equilibrium length an O–H bond behaves like a spring: $V(r)=\tfrac12 k_b (r-r_0)^2$. For the flexible model we use in this module (SPC/Fw, see Lesson 4) $k_b = 1059.162$ kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻² and $r_0 = 1.012$ Å. Two atoms joined by a spring oscillate with

$$ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k_b}{\mu}}, \qquad \mu = \frac{m_\mathrm{O}\, m_\mathrm{H}}{m_\mathrm{O}+m_\mathrm{H}} \approx 0.949\ \text{amu}, \qquad \tilde\nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi c}. $$

With these numbers $\tilde\nu \approx 3630$ cm⁻¹, a period of only about 9 fs. This is the fastest motion in water, and it decides how small the time step has to be: an integrator needs roughly ten steps per period (Module 2), so about 1 fs or less (flexible water is usually run with 0.5 fs). Real bonds are not perfectly harmonic: a Morse potential $D(1-e^{-a(r-r_0)})^2$ is closer to the truth and softens at large stretch. Force fields usually keep the harmonic form because the thermal motion of an O–H bond stays well inside the harmonic region.

You double the force constant $k_b$ of an O–H bond. What happens to its vibrational frequency?

  1. It doubles.
  2. It increases by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.
  3. It stays the same: only the amplitude changes.
$\omega = \sqrt{k_b/\mu}$, so the frequency grows with the square root of the stiffness. Doubling $k_b$ shortens the period by a factor of 1.41, and the largest stable time step shrinks by the same factor. Try it in the lab above.
Exercise 1 — How big a time step can a flexible water molecule take?

In the bond lab, keep the default force constant and the harmonic potential. Increase the time step from 0.25 fs upward. (a) Read the “largest energy error” for 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 fs: how does it scale with the time step? Where does it first exceed 1 %? (b) At which time step does the trajectory blow up? (c) How does your answer for (b) compare with the textbook stability limit $\Delta t < 2/\omega$ of Velocity Verlet?

What you should see

(a) The error is a bounded oscillation of $E(t)$ around $E(0)$ (not a drift) and it scales as $(\omega\Delta t)^2$: about 0.7 % at 0.25 fs, 2.9 % at 0.5 fs, 12 % at 1 fs and 47 % at 2 fs. It first exceeds 1 % between 0.25 and 0.5 fs. (b) and (c) The run stays bounded up to $\Delta t = 2/\omega \approx 2.93$ fs, exactly the limit the lab prints, and diverges above it (3 fs already blows up). In practice nobody runs near the stability limit: the dynamics is badly wrong long before it explodes. This is why flexible water is run with 0.25–0.5 fs steps, and why most production simulations replace the O–H bonds by constraints, which removes the fast motion and allows 2 fs.

Lesson 3The Angle Term and a Flexible H₂O Molecule

Three atoms joined by two bonds also resist bending: $V(\theta)=\tfrac12 k_\theta(\theta-\theta_0)^2$, with $\theta_0 = 113.24^\circ$ and $k_\theta = 75.90$ kcal mol⁻¹ rad⁻² for SPC/Fw. A bent triatomic molecule has three vibrations, the symmetric stretch (both O–H bonds lengthen together), the asymmetric stretch (one lengthens while the other shortens) and the bend (the angle opens and closes). The bend is much softer than the stretches, so it is slower.

We can find the frequencies two ways. Analytically, diagonalise the Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives of the energy); ASE does this for us with ase.vibrations.Vibrations, which differentiates the forces numerically. Dynamically, run a short MD simulation and Fourier transform a time series (the idea behind the vibrational spectra computed from MD). The next cell does the first.

ASE: vibrational modes of one SPC/Fw molecule (needs water_ase.py, see Lesson 5)
import numpy as np
from ase import Atoms, units
from ase.vibrations import Vibrations
from water_ase import Water3Site, MODELS

par = MODELS["spcfw"]
h = np.radians(par["theta"]) / 2
mol = Atoms("OHH", positions=[[0, 0, 0],
                              [par["rOH"] * np.sin(h), par["rOH"] * np.cos(h), 0],
                              [-par["rOH"] * np.sin(h), par["rOH"] * np.cos(h), 0]],
            cell=[24, 24, 24], pbc=True)
mol.center()
mol.calc = Water3Site("spcfw")
print(f"energy of the relaxed geometry: {mol.get_potential_energy():.6f} eV (bonded part = {mol.calc.parts['bond'] + mol.calc.parts['angle']:.6f} eV)")
vib = Vibrations(mol, name="tmp_vib", delta=0.01)
vib.run()
freqs = vib.get_frequencies()
for i, f in enumerate(freqs):
    print(f"mode {i}: {f.real:9.1f} cm^-1" if abs(f.imag) < 1e-6 else f"mode {i}: {f.imag:9.1f}i cm^-1")
vib.clean()

The first six “modes” are the three translations and three rotations of the free molecule, which should have zero frequency (the tiny values, a few cm⁻¹ to 24 cm⁻¹ here, come from the finite displacement and from the molecule seeing its own periodic images in a 24 Å cell). The three real modes are at about 1371 cm⁻¹ (bend) and 3587 and 3671 cm⁻¹ (symmetric and asymmetric stretch). For comparison, the gas-phase experimental values are about 1595, 3657 and 3756 cm⁻¹. SPC/Fw was parameterised for the liquid, where hydrogen bonding shifts the bend up and the stretches down, so it is not expected to match the gas phase exactly: the stretches are within 2.5 %, the bend about 14 % low.

Why does an H₂O molecule have exactly three vibrational modes?

  1. Because it has three atoms, and each atom contributes one vibration.
  2. Because it has two bonds and one angle, so every internal coordinate is its own mode.
  3. Because three atoms have $3 \times 3 = 9$ degrees of freedom, and removing 3 translations and 3 rotations leaves 3 vibrations.
$3N - 6$ for a non-linear molecule. In water the bond and angle coordinates happen to be close to the normal modes, but in general normal modes are combinations of internal coordinates: the symmetric stretch and the bend mix a little, which is why the lab can show a small peak of one in the time series of the other.

Lesson 4The Non-Bonded Part and the SPC Family

The bonded terms give the molecule its shape. What makes liquid water a liquid is the non-bonded part: partial charges on oxygen (negative) and hydrogens (positive) produce a molecular dipole and hydrogen bonds, and a Lennard-Jones interaction on the oxygens provides the short-range repulsion. This is the physics of Module 3 applied to three sites per molecule. A neutral molecule needs $q_\mathrm O = -2 q_\mathrm H$.

The simple point charge (SPC) model of Berendsen and co-workers uses a rigid tetrahedral geometry and the smallest possible set of ingredients. SPC/E (“extended”) raises the charges a little to account on average for the polarisation of a molecule in the liquid. SPC/Fw keeps the SPC charges and Lennard-Jones but makes the molecule flexible and re-fits the geometry.

Notice that the isolated water molecule has a dipole of only 1.85 D, while these models carry 2.2–2.35 D: an effective model has to work for water in water, where the neighbours polarise each molecule and the dipole is larger. SPC/E makes this explicit by raising the charges, at a price: the energy needed to polarise the molecule from 1.85 D to 2.35 D is not included in the simulation, so SPC/E’s computed potential energy is too negative, and the heat of vaporisation needs a correction (Module 12 does this calculation).

💡 Deep dive: electrostatics in these codes

Both the Python calculator and the browser engine use a plain Ewald sum (Module 3) for the electrostatics: a real-space sum of $q_i q_j\,\mathrm{erfc}(\alpha r)/r$ out to a cutoff, plus a reciprocal-space sum, a self term $-\alpha/\sqrt\pi \sum q^2$, and the exclusion correction of Lesson 1. Production codes use the faster particle-mesh version (PME). The Lennard-Jones interaction on oxygen is truncated and shifted at the same cutoff; the long-range tail correction is added only when comparing with experiment (Module 12).

Lesson 5Running Water in ASE

Rigid or flexible?

A flexible model has all three vibrations and needs 0.5 fs time steps. A rigid model freezes the two bond lengths and the angle, which removes the fast vibrations entirely: with the molecule rigid, the fastest remaining motion is the libration (hindered rotation) of the molecule, and a time step of 2 fs is safe. The price is that rigid models cannot describe the infrared spectrum or any property that depends on intramolecular vibration, and the three constraints must be satisfied at every step by an algorithm: SHAKE (Ryckaert et al. 1977 [4]) corrects positions after the Verlet step, RATTLE (Andersen 1983 [5]) also corrects velocities, and SETTLE (Miyamoto and Kollman 1992 [6]) solves the three constraints of a rigid water molecule analytically, which is why it is the default in production codes.

What ASE provides

ASE has two water calculators, ase.calculators.tip3p.TIP3P and ase.calculators.tip4p.TIP4P. They use a smooth molecule-based cutoff instead of Ewald and keep their parameters in module constants, so they cannot be used for SPC/E or SPC/Fw. Rigid molecules are made with ase.constraints.FixBondLengths. Everything else in ASE is generic: a force field is just a Calculator that returns an energy and forces. So we wrote one: water_ase.py (about 200 lines of NumPy) implements SPC, SPC/E and SPC/Fw with Ewald electrostatics. The core of the flexible model is only these lines:

# inside Water3Site.calculate(): bonded terms of the flexible model, vectorised over all molecules
kb, kt, r0, t0 = p["kb"], p["ktheta"], p["rOH"], np.radians(p["theta"])
v1, v2 = R[:, 1] - R[:, 0], R[:, 2] - R[:, 0]            # O->H1 and O->H2 for every molecule
r1, rb = np.linalg.norm(v1, axis=1), np.linalg.norm(v2, axis=1)
e1, e2 = v1 / r1[:, None], v2 / rb[:, None]
e_b = 0.5 * kb * ((r1 - r0) ** 2 + (rb - r0) ** 2).sum()  # bond energy
f1, f2 = -kb * (r1 - r0), -kb * (rb - r0)                 # bond forces
F[:, 1] += f1[:, None] * e1; F[:, 0] -= f1[:, None] * e1
F[:, 2] += f2[:, None] * e2; F[:, 0] -= f2[:, None] * e2
cth = np.clip((e1 * e2).sum(1), -0.999999, 0.999999); th = np.arccos(cth); sth = np.sqrt(1 - cth ** 2)
dth = th - t0; e_a = 0.5 * kt * (dth ** 2).sum()           # angle energy
pref = kt * dth / sth                                      # -dV/dcos(theta)
g1 = pref[:, None] * (e2 - cth[:, None] * e1) / r1[:, None]
g2 = pref[:, None] * (e1 - cth[:, None] * e2) / rb[:, None]
F[:, 1] += g1; F[:, 2] += g2; F[:, 0] -= g1 + g2

We checked the forces against finite differences (agreement to 10⁻⁹ eV/Å) and the energies against the independent JavaScript implementation on the same configurations. ASE’s own FixBondLengths loops over bonds in pure Python (seconds per step for a water box), so water_ase.py also contains RigidWater, a subclass that applies SHAKE/RATTLE to all molecules at once with NumPy.

A box of liquid water

The recipe is the same as for the LJ fluid in Module 8: build, minimise, assign velocities, run Langevin NVT. Two differences: the box holds molecules (placed on a lattice in the least clashing of 60 random orientations), and rigid models carry constraints. The system is 64 molecules at 0.997 g/cm³ (the density of water at 25 °C) and 300 K.

ASE: rigid SPC/E, 64 molecules, 2 fs steps — run as python m9_boxes.py spce 64 2.0 1500 150
import time, sys
import numpy as np
from ase import units
from ase.md.langevin import Langevin
from ase.md.velocitydistribution import MaxwellBoltzmannDistribution, Stationary
from ase.optimize import FIRE
from water_ase import Water3Site, water_box, rigid_constraint

model, nmol, dt_fs, n_steps, every = sys.argv[1], int(sys.argv[2]), float(sys.argv[3]), int(sys.argv[4]), int(sys.argv[5])
kJ = 96.4853                                    # eV -> kJ/mol
atoms = water_box(model, nmol, density_gcc=0.997, seed=11)
atoms.calc = Water3Site(model)
L = atoms.cell[0, 0]
print(f"{model}: {nmol} molecules, {len(atoms)} atoms, box {L:.3f} A, density {nmol * 18.01528 / 0.602214076 / L**3:.3f} g/cm^3")
if model != "spcfw":
    atoms.set_constraint(rigid_constraint(nmol, model))
print(f"E before minimisation: {atoms.get_potential_energy() / nmol * kJ:.2f} kJ/mol per molecule")
FIRE(atoms, logfile=None).run(fmax=0.3, steps=300)
print(f"E after minimisation:  {atoms.get_potential_energy() / nmol * kJ:.2f} kJ/mol per molecule, max |F| = {abs(atoms.get_forces()).max():.2f} eV/A")
MaxwellBoltzmannDistribution(atoms, temperature_K=300, rng=np.random.default_rng(3)); Stationary(atoms)

# stage 1: strong friction and a short step absorb the heat released while the lattice relaxes
Langevin(atoms, 0.5 * dt_fs * units.fs, temperature_K=300, friction=0.1 / units.fs, rng=np.random.default_rng(4)).run(int(0.4 * n_steps))
# stage 2: production-like run with weak friction
dyn = Langevin(atoms, dt_fs * units.fs, temperature_K=300, friction=0.01 / units.fs, rng=np.random.default_rng(5))
t0 = time.time()
def log():
    n = dyn.nsteps
    print(f"step {n:5d}  t = {n * dt_fs / 1000:5.2f} ps  Epot/molecule = {atoms.get_potential_energy() / nmol * kJ:7.2f} kJ/mol  T = {atoms.get_temperature():6.1f} K")
dyn.attach(log, interval=every)
dyn.run(n_steps)
print(f"done in {time.time() - t0:.0f} s ({(time.time() - t0) / n_steps * 1000:.0f} ms per step)")

Look at the logs. After minimisation the potential energy sits near −39 kJ/mol per molecule for SPC/E, and relaxes to about −45 kJ/mol per molecule while the thermostat holds 300 K (the instantaneous temperature of 64 rigid molecules fluctuates by about ±7 %). This is the energy of the hydrogen-bond network: each molecule takes part in about 3.5 hydrogen bonds, which works out at roughly 20–25 kJ/mol per bond. The flexible model prints a higher number, around −39 kJ/mol, because its potential energy includes the bond and angle energy of the vibrating molecules. Notice also the cost: the rigid run needed 4× fewer steps per picosecond, and ASE handled the constraints with the same Langevin class as before.

SPC/E uses larger charges than SPC (−0.8476 vs −0.82). What is the physical reason?

  1. The larger charges make the model more accurate for water vapour.
  2. They give the molecule the larger average dipole it has in the liquid, as a mean-field stand-in for polarisation.
  3. They compensate for using a cutoff in the Lennard-Jones term.
A free water molecule has a dipole of 1.85 D, but in the liquid the electric field of its neighbours polarises it to roughly 2.4–3 D. A fixed-charge model cannot respond to its environment, so SPC/E bakes an average liquid-phase dipole (2.35 D) into the charges. The cost is that the model overbinds in the gas phase and the polarisation energy must be subtracted when computing the heat of vaporisation.
Exercise 2 — Rigid versus flexible in the browser

In the water-box lab run SPC/E (rigid, 2 fs) and SPC/Fw (flexible, 0.5 fs) with 64 molecules and let each run for about 3 ps. Compare the first peak of $g_\mathrm{OO}(r)$, the mean dipole moment and the number of hydrogen bonds per molecule. What you should see

Both models put the first oxygen–oxygen peak at 2.77 Å (the O···O hydrogen-bond distance). Our runs gave a height of about 2.9 for SPC/E and a lower, broader peak of about 2.5 for SPC/Fw (rigid water is more sharply structured). SPC/E’s mean dipole is exactly 2.35 D because the molecule is rigid; SPC/Fw’s average is about 2.4 D, larger than the 2.19 D of its gas-phase geometry, because the molecule stretches and bends in the electric field of its neighbours: a flexible model polarises a little by itself. The number of hydrogen bonds donated per molecule is about 1.8 for SPC/E and 1.6 for SPC/Fw (3.2–3.6 counting both donating and accepting), as expected for a tetrahedral network. For temperature fluctuations, the canonical estimate is $\sqrt{2/N_f}$ with $N_f$ degrees of freedom: about 11 % for 27 rigid molecules ($N_f = 159$) and 5 % for 125 ($N_f = 747$).

End of Module 9. We built a water model from its parts:

  • Bonded terms (bond stretch and angle bend) and the time-step limit they impose through the fastest vibration.
  • Non-bonded terms: Lennard-Jones on oxygen and partial charges, with all intramolecular pairs excluded.
  • The SPC family: rigid SPC and SPC/E, flexible SPC/Fw; constraints (SHAKE/RATTLE/SETTLE).
  • A custom ASE Calculator and a vectorised constraint, used to run liquid water at 300 K.

📝 Key Takeaways

  • A molecular force field adds bonded terms (bonds, angles, dihedrals) to the non-bonded Lennard-Jones and Coulomb terms; non-bonded interactions inside a molecule are excluded.
  • The stiffest bond sets the time step: an O–H stretch has a period of about 9 fs, so flexible water needs 0.5–1 fs steps; rigid water (constraints) can use 2 fs.
  • SPC, SPC/E and SPC/Fw all use partial charges on O and H plus Lennard-Jones on O; SPC/E’s larger charges mimic liquid-phase polarisation.
  • In ASE, a force field is any Calculator; constraints are objects attached to the Atoms; the dynamics code is unchanged from Module 8.
