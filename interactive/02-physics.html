Lesson 1Classical Mechanics and Newton's Laws

At the core of MD simulations lies Classical Mechanics, specifically Newton's Second Law of Motion. For each atom $i$ in our system with mass $m_i$ and position vector $\mathbf{r}_i$, the law states:

$$ \mathbf{F}_i = m_i \mathbf{a}_i $$

where:

  • $\mathbf{F}_i$ is the total force acting on atom $i$ due to all other atoms in the system.
  • $\mathbf{a}_i$ is the acceleration of atom $i$.

Recall that acceleration is the second derivative of position with respect to time, $\mathbf{a}_i = \frac{d^2 \mathbf{r}_i}{dt^2}$, and the first derivative of velocity, $\mathbf{a}_i = \frac{d\mathbf{v}_i}{dt}$. So, we can rewrite Newton's second law as a second-order ordinary differential equation:

$$ \frac{d^2 \mathbf{r}_i}{dt^2} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_i(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{r}_N)}{m_i} $$

The force $\mathbf{F}_i$ depends on the positions of all atoms in the system ($\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{r}_N$).

The goal of MD

If we know the positions and velocities of all atoms at a certain time $t$, and we have a way to calculate the force $\mathbf{F}_i$ on each atom, we can use Newton's equations to predict the positions and velocities at a slightly later time $t + \Delta t$. By repeating this process over many small time steps $\Delta t$, we simulate the trajectory of the system over time.

This process of predicting the future state based on the current state is called numerical integration. We'll explore specific algorithms for this in Lesson 3.

Lesson 2Potential Energy and Force Fields

Where do the forces ($\mathbf{F}_i$) come from? In classical mechanics, forces often arise from a potential energy function, $V(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{r}_N)$, which depends on the positions of all particles in the system. This function defines the Potential Energy Surface (PES).

The force on atom $i$ is the negative gradient of the potential energy with respect to the coordinates of atom $i$:

$$ \mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_i} V = -\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i}, \frac{\partial V}{\partial y_i}, \frac{\partial V}{\partial z_i} \right) $$

This means if we can define the potential energy $V$, we can calculate the forces needed for Newton's equations.

At the particle's current position the slope of the potential is $dV/dx = +3$. What is the force on the particle?

  1. $F = +3$ — it is pushed toward $+x$, up the slope
  2. $F = -3$ — it is pushed toward $-x$, downhill
  3. $F = 0$ — forces only appear at the minima
$F = -dV/dx$, so a positive slope gives a negative force: the particle is always pushed downhill. The force is zero only where the slope is zero (minima, maxima such as the top of the double-well barrier).

Force fields

In molecular simulations, the potential energy function $V$ is called a Force Field (FF). It's an empirical model, meaning it's based on fitting mathematical functions to experimental data and/or quantum mechanical calculations. Force fields approximate the true quantum mechanical interactions with simpler classical functions, making calculations feasible for large systems.

A typical force field decomposes the total potential energy into several terms:

$$ V_{\text{total}} = V_{\text{bonded}} + V_{\text{non-bonded}} $$
1

Bonded terms

Describe interactions between atoms connected by covalent bonds (bond stretching, angle bending, dihedral torsion). These are typically short-ranged.

2

Non-bonded terms

Describe interactions between atoms that are not directly bonded (or are separated by several bonds).

van der Waals interactions (Lennard-Jones potential)

$$ V_{LJ}(r_{ij}) = 4\epsilon_{ij} \left[ \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^{6} \right] $$

This term decays relatively quickly (approximately as $r^{-6}$ in the attractive tail).

Electrostatic interactions (Coulomb potential)

$$ V_{\text{Coulomb}}(r_{ij}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} $$

This term decays more slowly (as $r^{-1}$).

Parameterization: The constants in these equations are the force field parameters, derived to reproduce experimental or QM data.

Lesson 3Integrating the Equations of Motion

Now that we know how to get forces from a potential ($F = -\nabla V$), how do we use them to update positions and velocities over time? We need a numerical integration algorithm to solve Newton's equations ($m\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{F}$).

A very simple approach is the Euler method, but it is generally not accurate or stable enough for MD. A much more popular and suitable algorithm is the Velocity Verlet algorithm.

Velocity Verlet algorithm

Given positions $\mathbf{r}$, velocities $\mathbf{v}$, accelerations $\mathbf{a}$, mass $m$, and time step $\Delta t$:

1

Half-step velocity update

$$ \mathbf{v}\left(t + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right) = \mathbf{v}(t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{a}(t)\,\Delta t $$

2

Position update

$$ \mathbf{r}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{r}(t) + \mathbf{v}\left(t + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\Delta t $$

3

Compute forces and new acceleration

$$ \mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t) = \frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t + \Delta t))}{m} $$

4

Half-step velocity completion

$$ \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{v}\left(t + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t)\,\Delta t $$

Notes
  • This scheme is time-reversible and conserves energy well for small $\Delta t$.
  • Choose $\Delta t$ to resolve the fastest motions (typically around 1–2 fs in atomistic MD).

The algorithm in code

Here is one Velocity Verlet step written as a Python function. The force routine is passed in as an argument, so the same integrator works for any potential.

def velocity_verlet_step(r_old, v_old, mass, dt, calculate_forces):
    """Performs one Velocity Verlet step with half-step velocity updates."""
    # 0. Compute force & acceleration at current position
    force_old = calculate_forces(r_old)
    a_old = force_old / mass

    # 1. Half-step velocity update
    v_half = v_old + 0.5 * a_old * dt

    # 2. Full position update using half-step velocity
    r_new = r_old + v_half * dt

    # 3. Compute force & acceleration at new position
    force_new = calculate_forces(r_new)
    a_new = force_new / mass

    # 4. Complete velocity update with new acceleration
    v_new = v_half + 0.5 * a_new * dt

    return r_new, v_new

Every row of this table can be checked with the lab below: the trajectory and phase-space panels show symplectic vs. non-symplectic behaviour, the energy panel shows conservation vs. drift, and the last panel measures the accuracy order directly. (Velocity Verlet costs one new force evaluation per step: the force at $\mathbf{r}(t+\Delta t)$ is reused as the “old” force of the next step.)

Halving the time step $\Delta t$ reduces the error of a fixed-length simulation by roughly what factor for Velocity Verlet?

  1. No change — the error only depends on the total simulated time
  2. A factor of 2 — like Euler
  3. A factor of 4 — it is second order in $\Delta t$
Velocity Verlet's global error scales as $\Delta t^2$, so halving $\Delta t$ cuts it by about $2^2 = 4$. Euler is first order: halving $\Delta t$ only halves its error. You can read both slopes off the log–log “accuracy order” panel.

⚠️ What goes wrong? Stability & time step selection

A critical parameter in any MD simulation is the time step ($dt$). If $dt$ is chosen too small, the simulation takes too long to cover physical time. If $dt$ is chosen too large, the integrator fails to resolve high-frequency atomic motions (like bond vibrations).

Let's run a demonstration showing what happens when $dt$ is set too large. We will see the total energy blow up exponentially, causing the system to explode!

Harmonic oscillator with a dangerously large time step 
# Let's simulate the harmonic oscillator with a dangerously large time step
dt_unstable = 0.8  # Dangerously large for k=1, mass=1 (normal period is ~6.28)
n_steps_unstable = 50

x_unst = np.zeros(n_steps_unstable)
v_unst = np.zeros(n_steps_unstable)
E_unst = np.zeros(n_steps_unstable)

# Initial state
x_unst[0] = 1.0
v_unst[0] = 0.0

mass = 1.0
k = 1.0

def force_h(x):
    return -k * x

x_curr, v_curr = x_unst[0], v_unst[0]
for i in range(1, n_steps_unstable):
    # Velocity Verlet step
    f_curr = force_h(x_curr)
    a_curr = f_curr / mass
    v_half = v_curr + 0.5 * a_curr * dt_unstable
    x_curr = x_curr + v_half * dt_unstable
    f_next = force_h(x_curr)
    a_next = f_next / mass
    v_curr = v_half + 0.5 * a_next * dt_unstable

    x_unst[i] = x_curr
    v_unst[i] = v_curr
    E_unst[i] = 0.5 * mass * v_curr**2 + 0.5 * k * x_curr**2

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4))
plt.plot(E_unst, 'r-o', label='Total Energy (Unstable dt)')
plt.xlabel('Step')
plt.ylabel('Energy')
plt.title('Energy Blow-up (Exploding Simulation) with dt = 0.8')
plt.yscale('log')  # Energy blows up exponentially!
plt.grid(True)
plt.legend()
plt.show()

The figure this code produces is drawn live in the lab below, with the same defaults (dt = 0.8, 50 steps, k = 1, mass = 1, log-scale energy axis).

Look closely at the default run

With Velocity Verlet and $dt = 0.8$ the energy of this oscillator fluctuates strongly (between about 0.42 and 0.50) but does not grow: for a harmonic oscillator, Velocity Verlet is stable as long as $\omega\,\Delta t < 2$, i.e. $\Delta t < 2/\omega = 2$ here. $dt = 0.8$ is still far too large for accuracy (only about 8 steps per period, against the rule of thumb of ≥ 10), but the true exponential blow-up appears once you push $dt$ past 2 — or immediately, at any $dt$, if you switch the lab to Euler, whose energy grows by a factor $1 + \omega^2\Delta t^2$ every step. (In the Python code above, E_unst[0] is never assigned and stays 0, which the log-scale plot silently drops; the lab plots the correct initial energy $E_0 = \tfrac12 k x_0^2 = 0.5$.)

Exercise — find the stability limit

Set $k = 4$ (with $m = 1$) in the stability lab. Predict the largest stable Velocity Verlet time step, then confirm it with the slider. What time step would the “1/10 of the fastest period” rule of thumb recommend?

Show solution

$\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = 2$, so Velocity Verlet is stable for $\Delta t < 2/\omega = 1$. The period is $2\pi/\omega = \pi \approx 3.14$, so the rule of thumb suggests $\Delta t \approx 0.31$ — about three times smaller than the stability limit. Stable is not the same as accurate.

Lesson 4Statistical Mechanics in MD

MD simulations generate microscopic information (positions, velocities). Statistical Mechanics connects this to macroscopic thermodynamic properties.

Thermodynamic ensembles: collections of microscopic states under macroscopic constraints.

Microcanonical (NVE)

Constant Number (N), Volume (V), Energy (E). Natural for basic Verlet.

Canonical (NVT)

Constant N, V, Temperature (T). Requires a thermostat.

Isothermal-Isobaric (NPT)

Constant N, Pressure (P), T. Requires a thermostat and barostat.

Temperature from kinetic energy

Total kinetic energy:

$$ E_K(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2} m_i \lvert\mathbf{v}_i(t)\rvert^2 $$

Temperature estimate:

$$ T = \frac{2E_K}{N_{\mathrm{df}} k_B} $$

Here, $N_{\mathrm{df}}$ is the number of degrees of freedom (for example, $3N$ for $N$ unconstrained particles in 3D), and $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant.

Play along: measuring temperature

A 3D simulation of $N = 100$ unconstrained atoms has a total kinetic energy $E_K = 150$ (reduced units, $k_B = 1$). What temperature does it report?

  1. $T = 1.0$, since $N_{\mathrm{df}} = 300$
  2. $T = 3.0$, since $N_{\mathrm{df}} = 100$
  3. $T = 1.5$, since $T = E_K / N$
Each atom has 3 velocity components, so $N_{\mathrm{df}} = 3N = 300$ and $T = 2 \times 150 / 300 = 1.0$.

Lesson 5Periodic Boundary Conditions

Simulating finite systems introduces unrealistic surface effects. To simulate bulk materials, we use Periodic Boundary Conditions (PBC).

Imagine the simulation box is replicated infinitely in all directions.

  • Forces include interactions with particles in neighboring image boxes.
  • When a particle exits one face, it re-enters the opposite face.

For an animation of particles crossing the box faces, see the PBC demo in Module 1. Below we look at the two practical questions PBC raises: which image of a neighbour to interact with, and how to wrap coordinates back into the box.

Lesson 6Minimum Image Convention and PBC in Practice

Minimum Image Convention (MIC): When calculating the interaction between two particles $i$ and $j$ under PBC, we must consider the interaction between particle $i$ and the closest periodic image of particle $j$.

  • Assume a box of lengths $L_x, L_y, L_z$.
  • Calculate the raw separation vector $\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)$.
  • Adjust each component using the nearest image distance:
    $$ \Delta x' = \Delta x - L_x \times \operatorname{round}(\Delta x / L_x) $$
    (Similarly for $y$ and $z$. The round() function finds the nearest integer.)
  • The distance used for force/potential calculation is $r_{ij} = |\Delta \mathbf{r}'|$.
  • This ensures we don't interact with a distant image when a closer one is available and avoids double-counting interactions (especially important with cutoffs).

In a box with $L_x = 10$, two particles have a raw separation $\Delta x = 7$. What separation does the minimum image convention use?

  1. $\Delta x' = 7$ — it is already inside the box
  2. $\Delta x' = 3$
  3. $\Delta x' = -3$
$\operatorname{round}(7/10) = 1$, so $\Delta x' = 7 - 10 = -3$: the nearest image of $j$ sits 3 units to the left of $i$, through the periodic boundary. The sign matters for the direction of the force.
Exercise — minimum image by hand

Show solution

Visualizing PBC

It's helpful to visualize how a particle “wraps around” the box. Imagine a 2D box:

  • If a particle's $x$ coordinate becomes greater than $L_x$, its new position might be $x' = x - L_x$.
  • If $x$ becomes less than $0$, its new position might be $x' = x + L_x$.

📝 Key Takeaways

  • Forces are derived as the negative gradient of the potential energy function ($F = -\nabla V(x)$).
  • Velocity Verlet is a second-order, symplectic integrator that achieves excellent long-term energy conservation (unlike the simple Euler method).
  • Time step stability requires that $dt$ be smaller than approximately 1/10 of the fastest vibrational period in the system ($dt \approx 1$–$2$ femtoseconds in real atomic systems containing hydrogen).
  • Setting the time step too large leads to exponential energy blow-up and simulation instability.
